Abstract— Emergency evacuation in institutional and campus facilities remains constrained by static signage and siloed building infrastructure. Modern facilities deploy heterogeneous Building Management Systems (BMS), Fire Alarm Systems (FAS), and Internet-of-Things (IoT) environmental sensors; however, differences in schema definitions, naming conventions, and telemetry payloads prevent these streams from forming a coherent, real-time spatial context. A recent structured literature survey conducted by our research team identified semantic interoperability across heterogeneous building subsystems as the primary bottleneck to closed-loop Digital Twin (DT) emergency management. This paper presents an AI-assisted semantic middleware designed to bridge heterogeneous BMS, FAS, and IoT data sources with a canonical building ontology inside a Digital Twin state manager. The primary scientific contribution is the semantic mapping architecture, which employs a hybrid similarity scoring and confidence-gating mechanism to map disparate source fields and spatial identifiers into an authoritative spatial graph while strictly segregating probabilistic data translation from deterministic, safety-critical path computation. We formalize the semantic mapping confidence metric and the time-dependent evacuation graph G_t = (V, E, W_t), in which hazard-compromised edges dynamically scale to infinite traversal cost. We detail the software prototype design—featuring a decoupled JSON-driven building model, an in-memory Digital Twin state manager, dynamic pathfinding, and a multi-panel 2D interactive interface. Furthermore, we establish an experimental evaluation protocol comparing dynamic Digital Twin routing against static nearest-exit baselines across reproducible hazard scenarios, formalize five explicit fail-safe fallback modes, and discuss empirical logging protocols. Consistent with scientific integrity, the paper documents a reproducible methodology and experimental framework; no unmeasured field benchmarks are claimed.

Keywords— Digital Twin; Semantic Interoperability; AI Middleware; Canonical Ontology; Emergency Evacuation; Dynamic Pathfinding; Graph Theory; Fail-Safe Systems; Building Information Modelling; Campus Safety.

I. INTRODUCTION

Modern academic and institutional campuses house complex building layouts occupied by high densities of transient occupants. During emergency events such as structure fires or hazardous material releases, static exit signs and pre-printed evacuation placards fail to account for evolving hazard distribution, localized smoke spread, or obstructed corridors. While modern facilities are increasingly equipped with sensory infrastructure—including Fire Alarm System (FAS) optical smoke detectors, Building Management System (BMS) zone temperature monitors and HVAC airflow controllers, and ad hoc Internet-of-Things (IoT) air-quality sensor networks—these subsystems typically operate within vendor-specific proprietary protocols and incompatible data representations.

In a prior structured literature survey [1], our research group evaluated twenty studies on Digital Twin (DT) emergency management and consolidated six fundamental research gaps (Table I). The most pervasive and architecturally critical barrier identified across existing implementations is the absence of automated semantic integration capable of unifying heterogeneous BMS, FAS, and IoT data streams into an authoritative spatial model in real time (Gap C). Secondary gaps include the architectural isolation of accessibility constraints from dynamic routing engines (Gap E) and the lack of multi-hazard validation (Gap B).

The core research contribution of this paper is an AI-assisted semantic interoperability framework designed to resolve schema fragmentation across disparate building subsystems without compromising the strict determinism required for life-safety routing. Rather than treating semantic data integration as an assumed prerequisite or conflating the contribution with graphical rendering or basic shortest-path algorithms, this work separates the system into distinct operational tiers: (i) probabilistic AI-assisted semantic field and entity mapping; (ii) deterministic confidence gating and schema validation; (iii) authoritative Digital Twin spatial state management; (iv) deterministic hazard assessment and dynamic graph pathfinding; (v) agent-based evacuation simulation; and (vi) decoupled 2D visual representation.

The key contributions of this paper are:

C1 (Semantic Middleware): A hybrid semantic mapping formulation combining semantic similarity with deterministic heuristic rules, governed by an explicit confidence threshold to prevent unverified telemetry from corrupting the authoritative Digital Twin state.

C2 (Mathematical Formulation): Formal representation of the dynamic building graph Gt = (V, E, Wt) and time-dependent edge traversal weight functions governed by real-time hazard severity.

C3 (Decoupled Implementation Architecture): A software prototype framework featuring a JSON-driven building topology model that decouples facility geometry and sensor topologies from core simulation logic, enabling extensibility across different architectural layouts.

C4 (Experimental Evaluation Protocol): A controlled experimental benchmark protocol designed to evaluate semantic mapping accuracy, entity resolution, computational latency, and dynamic vs. static baseline evacuation performance under identical fire propagation conditions.

C5 (Fail-Safe Safety Architecture): A safety-first architecture with five explicit fail-safe boundaries designed to ensure predictable fallback states under schema ambiguity, sensor conflict, network desynchronization, or egress graph disconnection.

II. RELATED WORK AND RESEARCH GAPS

A. Digital Twin Architectures in Life Safety

Digital Twins have emerged as an active paradigm for synchronizing physical built environments with real-time digital replicas. Prior works have formulated closed-loop DT workflows comprising multimodal sensing, state synchronisation, artificial intelligence analytics, dynamic egress calculation, and actuation feedback [2]. Researchers have evaluated these frameworks across airport terminals, transit tunnels, and sports arenas using Integration-Readiness (IRL) and Technology-Readiness (TRL) frameworks [3]. However, as documented in [2] and [3], existing implementations typically depend on pre-engineered, homogeneous data pipelines where field names, unit conversions, and device IDs are hard-coded during system commissioning.

B. Dynamic Evacuation Guidance and Perception

Controlled virtual reality (VR) experiments demonstrate that dynamic directional exit signage informed by real-time hazard data can reduce evacuation delay and occupant hesitation compared to traditional static signage [4], [5]. Eye-tracking studies in simulated smoke environments further emphasize that dynamic path indications must adapt rapidly as smoke blankets primary egress paths [5]. Computer-vision pipelines utilizing YOLO architectures have reported sub-90 ms physical-to-twin synchronisation [6]. Nonetheless, these perception layers uniformly presume that underlying environmental data arrives in a uniform canonical format.

C. Crowd Simulation and Accessibility Scoping

Large Language Model (LLM)-steered agent-based simulations have recently demonstrated natural language control over multi-thousand occupant scenarios [7], while synthetic agent populations have modelled disaster communication and evacuation responses during extreme weather events [8]. Assistive mobile systems have also been formulated for individuals with visual impairments during structural fires [9]. Across current literature, accessibility adaptations and crowd behavioral models typically operate as isolated downstream simulators rather than being integrated directly into the authoritative routing engine.

D. Research Gap Positioning

Our structured survey [1] consolidated the state of the art into six clear research gaps (Table I). Among these, Gap C—the absence of automated, live semantic integration across heterogeneous BMS, FAS, IoT, and BIM-IFC data sources—constitutes the foundational architectural roadblock. Without automated semantic mapping, a Digital Twin cannot scale across buildings containing multi-vendor equipment. This paper directly addresses Gap C as its primary contribution, while architecting the underlying graph model such that Gap E (accessibility-aware routing) can be accommodated as a graph constraint in future work.

III. PROBLEM STATEMENT AND DESIGN OBJECTIVES

Problem Statement: Modern educational and commercial facilities deploy disparate sensory systems that represent identical spatial locations and environmental phenomena using incompatible syntactic schemas, divergent identifier formats (e.g., numeric zone codes vs. loop addresses vs. free-text MQTT topics), and inconsistent engineering units. Existing emergency management systems require manual data integration during building setup. No current system provides an automated, AI-assisted semantic mapping layer that continuously resolves heterogeneous telemetry into an authoritative Digital Twin state while maintaining deterministic, fail-safe evacuation routing and providing a decoupled, floor-plan-accurate 2D operational interface.

To resolve this problem, five measurable design objectives are established:

O1 (Canonical Ontology Definition): Formulate an extensible building ontology encompassing rooms, corridors, exit portals, sensors, hazard thresholds, and connectivity topology inspired by IFC and RDF/OWL standards.

O2 (AI Semantic Middleware): Construct a hybrid AI-assisted mapping pipeline combining semantic embedding similarity and heuristic rules, coupled with a confidence gating filter to quarantine low-confidence field associations.

O3 (Decoupled Building Digital Twin): Develop an in-memory Digital Twin state manager driven by an external JSON building configuration that decouples physical floor geometry from rendering logic.

O4 (Dynamic Evacuation Routing & Simulation): Implement time-dependent graph pathfinding (A* and Dijkstra) that penalizes degraded transit corridors and severs hazardous edges, driving a multi-agent evacuation simulator.

O5 (Experimental Benchmark & Safety Architecture): Establish an empirical evaluation protocol measuring mapping accuracy, entity resolution, computational latency, and dynamic-vs-static evacuation performance alongside five fail-safe safety boundaries.

IV. PROPOSED SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

The proposed framework is structured into six modular, decoupled operational tiers (Fig. 1). A key architectural principle is the strict boundary between probabilistic data ingestion and deterministic life-safety execution.

1) Sensing & Ingestion Layer: Simulates or ingests multi-source building telemetry from three heterogeneous streams: (a) BMS publishing zone-based temperatures and HVAC metrics; (b) FAS publishing loop-based discrete smoke alarm packets; and (c) ad hoc IoT environmental sensors publishing JSON telemetry over free-text MQTT topics.

2) AI Semantic Middleware Tier: Consumes unstandardized telemetry payloads, performs concept matching and heuristic normalization against the canonical ontology, computes an association confidence score, and enforces confidence gating before state admission.

3) Digital Twin State Manager: Serves as the authoritative single source of truth, maintaining synchronized spatial states (temperature, smoke optical density, occupant counts, hazard ratings) for every defined room and corridor node.

4) Hazard Engine & Routing Engine: The hazard engine deterministically applies life-safety threshold logic (ASET/RSET criteria) to classify zones into SAFE, DEGRADED, or UNSAFE. The dynamic routing engine operates on the building topology graph G_t, updating edge traversal costs in real time and recomputing optimal egress vectors.

5) Occupant Agent Simulator: Evaluates individual occupant agent state machines, executing real-time trajectory updates as routes adapt and flagging trapped agents when no safe path exists.

6) Presentation & Metrics Engine: Delivers an interactive 2D floor-plan visualization via WebSockets while structured event loggers record mapping precision, routing latency, and evacuation progression metrics.

VII. CLOSED-LOOP SIMULATION WORKFLOW

Fig. 3 illustrates the execution cycle executed per simulation tick (configured nominally at 100 ms intervals / 10 Hz in the prototype simulation runner).

1) Tick Trigger & Scenario Injection: The scenario engine injects scheduled or user-actuated hazard parameters (ignition zone, growth rate, spread coefficients).

2) Synthetic Telemetry Generation: Synthetic sensor nodes emit heterogeneous payloads containing simulated thermal values and smoke concentrations.

3) Semantic Mapping & Validation: Ingested telemetry is processed by the AI middleware; valid mappings update the state manager while sub-threshold payloads are quarantined.

4) Hazard Classification: Authoritative temperature and smoke levels are evaluated against deterministic tenability thresholds.

5) Graph Recalculation: If hazard states transition, the routing engine updates edge weights W_t and recomputes paths over G_t.

6) Agent Trajectory Execution: Occupant agents step forward along active paths; agents encountering severed paths adopt newly computed safe paths.

7) UI Dispatch & Metrics Logging: State vectors are serialized over WebSocket to the browser client and committed to event logs.

VIII. AI-ASSISTED SEMANTIC MIDDLEWARE METHODOLOGY

The semantic middleware directly targets Gap C by mapping disparate subsystem representations onto the canonical ontology. Three distinct synthetic schemas are generated to reflect common vendor variations:

• BMS Payload: Keyed by numeric zone integers (e.g., zone_id: 104), temperature in Celsius (temp_deg_c: 68.4), and integer occupant counts (occ_val: 12).

• FAS Payload: Keyed by alarm panel loop descriptors (e.g., loop_addr: "L1-D04"), binary smoke flags (smoke_alarm: 1), and heat rise thresholds (rate_of_rise: true).

• IoT Payload: Keyed by hierarchical MQTT topic strings containing informal text (e.g., topic: "campus/floor2/room_204/air_quality"), float optical density (opt_smoke_od: 0.84), and ambient temperature in Fahrenheit (ambient_temp_f: 155.1).

A. Entity Matching and Spatial Disambiguation

Beyond field-level schema matching, the middleware performs spatial entity resolution. Real-world installations exhibit naming variations: Room 204 may appear as "R204", "ROOM-204", "room_204", "zone-204", or "Lab1-Floor2". The entity resolution pipeline uses regular expression extractors and alias lookups to normalize these variations to the uniform ontological entity URI: Campus:Floor2:Room204.

B. Confidence Gating & Safety Isolation

When an ingested field fails to achieve the configured prototype confidence threshold τ = 0.75 in Eq. (2), the middleware quarantines the mapping. The system retains the last known-good value in the Digital Twin state, logs a low-confidence telemetry event, and notifies the operator interface. This design prevents ambiguous schema associations from propagating into spatial hazard classifications.

IX. OCCUPANT SIMULATION AND HAZARD REASONING

Simulated occupants are instantiated as discrete autonomous agents assigned to rooms based on room capacity parameters. Each agent implements a five-state finite state machine:

1) IDLE: Occupant engaged in normal room activity prior to alarm activation.

2) AWARE: Occupant alerted to emergency, undergoing pre-evacuation response delay (nominally modeled with log-normal distribution μ = 3.0 s, σ = 1.0 s in the prototype).

3) EVACUATING: Occupant actively traversing designated shortest path Pt*(s, exit) at individual walking speed vi ~ 𝒰(1.1, 1.4) m/s.

4) REROUTED: Occupant whose active path intercepted a newly compromised hazard node, pivoting to an alternate computed safe path.

5) EVACUATED / BLOCKED: Terminal states indicating successful exit arrival or entrapment due to complete graph disconnection.

Hazard classifications follow rule-based tenability thresholds configured in the prototype: temperature T < 55 °C and smoke optical density OD < 0.5 m⁻¹ is classified as SAFE; 55 °C ≤ T < 70 °C or 0.5 ≤ OD < 1.0 m⁻¹ is DEGRADED; T ≥ 70 °C or OD ≥ 1.0 m⁻¹ is UNSAFE. Consistent with our prototype scoping (Section XIII), agents currently follow rational shortest paths; complex crowd panic, bottleneck crushing, or irrational counter-flow represent future extensions.

XIII. RESULTS DISCUSSION AND THREATS TO VALIDITY

A. Analytical and Expected System Behavior

The primary design principle demonstrated by this framework is that semantic interoperability across heterogeneous building subsystems can be resolved in real time while maintaining deterministic safety guarantees. By separating the probabilistic AI mapping layer from the deterministic spatial state manager, the system is designed to prevent machine learning inference uncertainties from directly influencing path calculation. The confidence gating filter (Eq. 2) ensures that only validated telemetry influences the dynamic edge weights Wt in Eq. (3). Under simulated conditions, the routing engine prevents traversal of graph edges classified as unsafe by setting Wt(u, v) → ∞ upon hazard confirmation.

B. Status of Experimental Execution

Consistent with the prototype status of this paper, automated logging routines have been architected as described in Section XI, while full experimental execution across repeated physical sensor trials remains pending. The benchmark template in Table III provides the formal protocol for recording empirical measurements once the software prototype undergoes extended batch execution.

C. Threats to Validity and Scope Boundaries

We explicitly articulate the boundary conditions of this research:

1) Synthetic Telemetry Scope: Experiments are formulated around synthetic sensor streams rather than physical building hardware deployments. While simulated noise profiles mirror common sensor variations, physical sensor drift, RF attenuation, and packet loss require future pilot validation.

2) Behavioral Rationality Assumption: Occupant agents follow assigned paths deterministically without modeling psychological panic, group stampeding, or irrational counter-flow.

3) Regulatory Certification: The system represents an engineering prototype framework; real-world deployment in municipal facilities requires adherence to relevant building fire safety and life-safety codes (e.g., NFPA / EN 54).

XIV. EXPECTED CONTRIBUTIONS AND IMPACT

This work delivers five main contributions to smart building safety engineering:

1) Semantic Middleware Architecture: Formulates cross-schema integration as an active architectural tier rather than a static design-time assumption (directly targeting Gap C).

2) Mathematical Rigor: Provides formal formulations for hybrid semantic association, confidence gating, and time-dependent graph penalization.

3) Extensible Decoupled Architecture: Demonstrates that floor plan geometry and sensor topologies can be decoupled via JSON configurations, supporting zero-code adaptability across facilities.

4) Reproducible Experimental Protocol: Establishes a transparent evaluation framework and baseline comparison protocol for empirical validation.

5) Fail-Safe Safety Architecture: Formalizes five deterministic fail-safe boundaries that define safe system responses during anomalous operating conditions.

XV. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

While this work establishes a comprehensive framework for semantic Digital Twin evacuation, several avenues for future research remain:

1) Physical IoT Pilot Deployment: Future work will deploy physical ESP32-based environmental sensing nodes across a live campus wing to evaluate middleware performance against live wireless telemetry.

2) Accessibility-Aware Dynamic Routing (Gap E): We plan to extend graph Gt with mobility-constrained edge attributes (e.g., wheelchair ramp vs. stairwell availability), enabling personalized evacuation vectors for occupants with disabilities.

3) Non-Linear Multi-Hazard Modeling (Gap B): Integrating coupled fire-and-structural-collapse simulations to evaluate system resilience under cascading disaster events.

4) High-Fidelity Crowd Dynamics: Incorporating social force models and panic-driven agent behaviors to evaluate crowd bottlenecking at exit portals.

XVI. CONCLUSION

Addressing the foundational gap identified in our recent structured survey [1], this paper has presented an AI-driven semantic middleware framework for an interactive 2D Digital Twin emergency evacuation system. By formulating hybrid semantic mapping, confidence gating, and dynamic graph pathfinding over Gt = (V, E, Wt), the architecture is designed to unify heterogeneous BMS, FAS, and IoT feeds into an authoritative spatial model without compromising deterministic life-safety execution. The modular JSON-driven prototype decouples facility architecture from simulation logic, while our comparative evaluation framework and five fail-safe fallback modes establish a sound foundation for intelligent, real-time emergency guidance in campus environments.
